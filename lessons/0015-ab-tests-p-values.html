Lesson 0015 · Stage 7: Uncertainty

Did it really work?

A/B tests are how businesses actually prove cause. The p-value answers one narrow question: could plain luck explain this gap?

⏱ 25–30 minutes, works on a phone ✎ No calculation, all reading and reasoning 🎯 Win: read a p-value correctly and spot the three classic A/B test mistakes
New words
A/B test
Randomly show half your visitors version A and half version B, then compare results.
Null hypothesis
The boring starting assumption: "there's no real difference between A and B".
p-value
If there really were no difference, the chance of seeing a gap at least this big by luck alone.
Statistically significant
p is below an agreed cut-off, usually 0.05. Luck alone is an unlikely explanation.
False positive
A "significant" result when there's really no difference. A false alarm.

Why A/B tests prove cause

In Lesson 0010, correlation couldn't prove cause because of confounders. An A/B test fixes that with a random split. A coin flip decides who sees B, so both groups have the same mix of big and small spenders, phone and laptop users, everything.

If the groups then behave differently, the only systematic difference left is the version they saw. That's what makes it a controlled experiment.

The catch: luck

Even two identical pages won't get exactly the same conversion rate. Random visitors bounce around, as the samples did in Lesson 0013. So when B beats A by 0.6 points, the question is: real, or just the bounce?

The p-value, in one sentence

"If A and B were truly the same, how often would luck alone produce a gap at least this big?"

  • Small p (e.g. 0.01): luck would rarely do this. We call the result statistically significant.
  • Big p (e.g. 0.40): luck easily does this. We haven't shown a difference. That's not the same as showing there's none.
  • The usual cut-off is 0.05: a 1-in-20 chance of a gap that big from luck.

What the p-value is NOT

Common misreadingReality
"p = 0.03 means a 97% chance B is better"No. It's the chance of the data assuming no difference, not the chance of the hypothesis.
"Significant means big"No. With millions of visitors, a tiny 0.05-point gap can be significant and still not worth anything.
"Not significant means no effect"No. The test may simply be too small to tell. Check the interval for the gap.

Try it

You set the true conversion rates, which in real life you never know. Each run simulates a test and reports what an analyst would see.

Experiments

  1. Pick No real difference, 2,000 visitors. Press Run it 20 times several times. About what share of runs come out "significant"? These are all false positives.
  2. Pick Small real lift (5% → 6%), 2,000 visitors. Run 20 times. How often does the test miss a lift that's really there?
  3. Same lift, switch to 10,000 visitors. How often does it catch it now?
  4. Pick Big lift (5% → 8%) with only 200 visitors. Even big effects can hide in tiny tests.

Three classic mistakes

  1. Peeking. Checking every day and stopping the first time p dips below 0.05. Results bounce, so if you check often enough, you'll catch a lucky dip. Decide the sample size up front.
  2. Testing 20 things. Compare 20 metrics with no real difference and, on average, one will be "significant" by luck (1 in 20). Pick the main metric before the test.
  3. Too small. Small tests miss real effects. "No significant difference" from 200 visitors tells you almost nothing.

Where you'll meet this

In Excel: for comparing two averages (such as order values), =T.TEST(A2:A500, B2:B500, 2, 3) returns a p-value (Microsoft Support: T.TEST). The 2 means "two-tailed" (a difference in either direction) and the 3 means the groups can have different spreads. For conversion rates, analysts usually use an online A/B test calculator or the testing tool's own report.

In Power BI: tests are run in other tools, but the results often land in dashboards. You'll read "uplift", "p-value", or "confidence" columns, and need to say what they mean.

In interviews: "Our A/B test showed B up 0.2 points, p = 0.01, on 3 million visitors. Should we ship it?" A strong answer: "It's very likely a real effect, but it's small. I'd check what 0.2 points is worth in revenue against the cost of building and maintaining B. Also, was the sample size fixed in advance, and was conversion the main metric we planned to test?"

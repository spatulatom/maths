Lesson 0008 · Stage 5: Spread & shape

How far from average?

Standard deviation: the spread number that goes with the mean, what it means in plain words, and which Excel function to use.

⏱ 20–25 minutes, works on a phone ✎ Pen and paper for one question, the rest is reasoning 🎯 Win: explain standard deviation in one sentence, and know when to use it instead of the IQR
New words
Deviation
How far one value is from the mean (the everyday average): value − mean. It can be negative.
Standard deviation (SD)
Roughly, the typical distance of a value from the mean. Small SD: values bunch together. Big SD: they're scattered.
Variance
The SD before taking the square root. You'll see the word, but you'll rarely report it.
Population / sample
Population: every value you care about (all 12 stores). Sample: a subset standing in for a bigger group (200 surveyed customers out of 10,000).
Bell-shaped
Data that piles up in the middle and thins out evenly on both sides. Also called a normal distribution.

A partner for the mean

In Lesson 0007, the IQR measured spread around the median. Standard deviation measures spread around the mean. Remember the two couriers, both averaging 5 days:

CourierDaysMeanSD
A4, 5, 5, 5, 650.63
B1, 2, 5, 8, 953.16

In plain words: courier A's deliveries are usually well under a day away from the average, while courier B's are usually about 3 days away. That one sentence is how you explain standard deviation to a manager.

How it's calculated

You won't do this by hand at work, since Excel does it. But seeing the steps once makes the number make sense. Take 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9 (OpenIntro Statistics, §2.1):

  1. Mean: 40 ÷ 8 = 5.
  2. Deviations (value − mean): −3, −1, −1, −1, 0, 0, 2, 4.
  3. Square each one: 9, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 4, 16. They add up to 32.
  4. Average the squares: 32 ÷ 8 = 4. This is the variance.
  5. Square root: √4 = 2. That's the standard deviation.
Why square, then square-root?

Add up the deviations in step 2 and you get 0. You always will, because the values above the mean exactly balance the ones below. Squaring makes every distance positive so nothing cancels out. The square root at the end brings the answer back to the original units (days, not "days squared").

Two versions in Excel

Step 4 has a twist. If your data is a sample, you divide by n − 1 instead of n (32 ÷ 7 instead of 32 ÷ 8). This corrects for the fact that a sample tends to underestimate the spread of the whole group. Excel has one function for each (Microsoft Support: STDEV.S):

Your data is…UseDivides byExample above
every value you care about=STDEV.P(range)n2
a sample of a bigger group=STDEV.S(range)n − 12.14

With hundreds of rows, the two answers are almost the same. What matters is that you can say which one you used and why.

The 68–95–99.7 rule

For bell-shaped data, the SD tells you where most values are (Lumen Learning: The Empirical Rule):

  • About 68% of values are within 1 SD of the mean.
  • About 95% are within 2 SD.
  • About 99.7% are within 3 SD.

Example: if call-centre wait times are bell-shaped with mean 10 minutes and SD 2, then about 95% of callers wait between 6 and 14 minutes (10 ± 2 × 2). Only use this rule when the data really is roughly bell-shaped. With skewed data like salaries or order values, the percentages are wrong.

Experiments

  1. Change the 9 to 30. How much does the SD jump? Last lesson, the IQR ignored an outlier like this. The SD can't, because squaring makes big distances count extra.
  2. Make every value the same, for example 5, 5, 5, 5. What's the SD? Why?
  3. Add 10 to every value (12, 14, 14, 14, 15, 15, 17, 19). The mean moves. Does the SD?
  4. Double every value (4, 8, 8, 8, 10, 10, 14, 18). What happens to the SD?

SD or IQR?

They're both measures of spread, but each one goes with its own kind of centre:

Data looks likeCentreSpread
Roughly symmetric, no wild values (test scores, heights, package weights)MeanStandard deviation
Skewed or has outliers (salaries, house prices, order values)MedianIQR

Same reasoning as Lesson 0002: the mean and SD both get pulled around by extreme values, while the median and IQR don't.

Where you'll meet this

In Excel: =STDEV.S(B2:B201) or =STDEV.P(B2:B13). The old STDEV function still works, but Microsoft recommends the newer two.

In Power BI: when you profile a numeric column in Power Query, the column statistics include the average and the standard deviation (Microsoft Learn: Profile data in Power BI). By default it looks at only the first 1,000 rows. That's the "Evaluate data, including data statistics" skill on the PL-300. DAX preview, nothing to memorise: STDEV.S(Orders[Days]) works the same way as in Excel.

In interviews: "Explain standard deviation to someone non-technical." A strong answer: "It's roughly how far a typical value is from the average. Small means consistent, big means all over the place."

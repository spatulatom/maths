Lesson 0011 · Stage 6: Relationships

Drawing the line

In Lesson 0010, r told you that distance and time move together. Now draw a line through the dots and read it as a rule: about 9 minutes to start, plus about 2 minutes for every kilometre. Then learn when to stop trusting it.

⏱ 25–30 minutes, works on a phone ✎ Light arithmetic: one multiply, one add 🎯 Win: read a trend line, make a safe prediction, and explain r²
New words
Trend line
The straight line that best fits the dots on a scatter plot. Also called the line of best fit, a regression line, or a linear trend.
Intercept
Where the line starts: its value when the across-number is 0. Like "9 minutes at 0 km".
Slope
How much the line climbs (or falls) for each 1 step to the right. The "per unit" number, like "2.1 minutes per km".
Gap (residual)
How far one dot sits above or below the line.
Extrapolation
Using the line to predict outside the range of your data. Risky.
r² (r-squared)
r multiplied by itself. Roughly, the share of the ups and downs in Y that the line accounts for.

But r doesn't answer the practical question: how many extra minutes does each extra kilometre cost? A trend line does. It's the one straight line that sits closest to all eight dots at once:

The line is a summary of the dots, the way the mean summarises one column. It turns eight separate deliveries into one rule you can use.

Reading the line: a start and a step

Every straight line is made of two numbers: where it starts, and how steeply it climbs. For the deliveries, the line is:

Time ≈ 9 + 2.1 × km

PartValueIn delivery terms
Intercept (the start)9At 0 km the line says 9 minutes. Think of it as the time every delivery costs anyway: parking, finding the door, handing over.
Slope (the step)2.1Each extra km goes with about 2.1 more minutes. So 10 extra km goes with about 21 more minutes.

Every straight trend line follows the same pattern. Y is the column on the up-axis (what you want to predict), and X is the column across:

Y ≈ intercept + slope × X

A slope can be negative, and then the line goes down. If a shop finds Units sold ≈ 500 − 4 × Price, each £1 added to the price goes with about 4 fewer units. A £10 rise goes with about 40 fewer.

Making a prediction

To use the rule, put a distance in and do the sum. Multiply first, then add:

DistanceSumLine says
10 km9 + 2.1 × 10 = 9 + 21about 30 min
15 km9 + 2.1 × 15 = 9 + 31.5about 40 min

There's no 10 km delivery in the data, but the line fills the gap. On the chart, that's going up from 10 km to the line, then across to the time.

Now try 60 km. The sum still works: 9 + 2.1 × 60 = 135 minutes. But the data only covers 3 to 25 km. At 60 km the driver is probably on a motorway or taking a break, so the rule from short city trips may not hold.

Predicting outside your data is called extrapolation. Give the number if someone asks, but say it's a guess. The intercept has the same problem: nobody made a 0 km delivery, so treat "9 minutes" as where the line starts, not a measured fact.

How the line is chosen: the smallest gaps

Why this line and not a slightly steeper one? Look at the gaps: how far each dot sits above or below the line. Statisticians call them residuals.

For our eight deliveries the gaps are tiny (4 minutes at most), so here is a messier week of eight deliveries, where they're easy to see:

The method tools use is called least squares (OpenIntro Statistics, Ch. 8):

  1. Measure each dot's gap to the line.
  2. Square each gap. Gaps above and below then both count, and big misses count extra.
  3. Add the squares up.
  4. Pick the line with the smallest total.

Squaring should feel familiar: standard deviation in Lesson 0008 did the same thing. You'll never do this by hand. Excel and Power BI find the line for you.

One odd delivery can tilt the line

Because big gaps count extra, one extreme point pulls hard. Suppose a ninth delivery, I, is 22 km but gets stuck in a traffic jam and takes 120 minutes:

Without IWith I
Slope2.1 min per km3.2 min per km
Intercept9 min1.3 min
r²0.980.60

One unusual trip changed the "rule" for all the others. That's why you look at the plot first and ask whether an odd point belongs in the data (a one-off jam) or tells you something real.

r²: how far to trust the line

r² is r × r, so it's always between 0 and 1. For the deliveries, r = 0.99, so r² = 0.98.

A common reading: about 98% of the differences in delivery time line up with distance. The other 2% is everything else: traffic, stairs, a chatty customer.

Compare the eight deliveries with the messier week from the gaps picture. The line comes out almost the same, but the dots don't stay close to it:

In the messy week, a 10 km prediction still says about 30 minutes, but real trips could easily take 15 or 50. A low r² doesn't make the line wrong. It makes it loose: a rough average, not a reliable rule.

Excel and Power BI show r² next to trend lines, usually written R². Higher means the dots hug the line more closely. It says nothing about cause: the slope is a pattern, not a promise, and all of Lesson 0010's warnings still apply.

Where you'll meet this

In Excel: right-click the dots on a scatter chart → Add Trendline, then tick "Display equation" and "Display R-squared". Or use formulas. With the distances in A2:A9 and the times in B2:B9:

FormulaGivesHere
=SLOPE(B2:B9, A2:A9)The slope2.13
=INTERCEPT(B2:B9, A2:A9)The intercept8.96
=RSQ(B2:B9, A2:A9)r²0.98
=FORECAST.LINEAR(10, B2:B9, A2:A9)Prediction at 10 km30.3

Watch the order: the Y range (what you predict) comes first, then the X range. Swap them and you get a different, wrong slope (Microsoft Support: SLOPE).

In Power BI: select a scatter or line chart and open the Analytics pane (the magnifying glass icon) to add a trend line. Line charts with a date axis also offer a Forecast option (Microsoft Learn: Analytics pane). The PL-300 exam lists "reference lines, error bars, and forecasting" by name.

In interviews: "Our trend line says sales will hit £2 million next year. Do you trust it?" A strong answer: "It depends how far it reaches past our data and how well the line fits, so I'd check R². I'd also ask what could change, like a new competitor or saturation, since a straight line assumes the past pattern continues."

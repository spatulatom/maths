Lesson 0012 · Stage 7: Uncertainty

What are the chances?

Probability for analysts: four rules that cover conversion rates, funnels, and "what's this worth per customer?"

⏱ 20–30 minutes, works on a phone ✎ Light arithmetic: multiply and add decimals 🎯 Win: multiply a funnel, handle overlaps, and compute revenue per visitor
New words
Probability
How likely something is, from 0 (never) to 1 (certain). 0.25 = 25% = "1 in 4".
Complement
The chance something does not happen: 1 minus the chance it does.
Independent
One event doesn't change the chance of the other.
Funnel
A chain of steps customers go through (visit → view → basket → pay), losing some at each step.
Expected value
Chance × value: what something is worth on average, per try.

You already use probability

A conversion rate of 3% is a probability: any one visitor has a 0.03 chance of buying. A churn rate of 8% is the chance a customer leaves this month. Stage 7 is about reading numbers like these with the right amount of trust.

Probability is written three ways that mean the same thing: 0.25, 25%, and 1 in 4. For calculations, always use the decimal.

Four rules

The overlap rule stops you counting the "both" customers twice. You'll see the same idea as a Venn diagram in the joins lesson.

Why AND multiplies

Start with 10,000 visitors. 40% view a product, which is 4,000 people. 25% of those add it to the basket, which is 1,000 people. That's 1,000 of the original 10,000, or 10%.

"25% of 40%" means multiplying, just as "percent of" did in stage 2. Each step takes a percentage of what's left, not of the starting crowd.

Watch out

Multiplying assumes each chance is measured among the people who passed the previous step. That's how funnel reports are usually built, so check the report says "% of previous step" and not "% of all visitors".

Expected value: chance × value

If 3% of visitors buy, and the average order is £60, then each visitor is worth 0.03 × £60 = £1.80 on average. Nobody spends exactly £1.80. It's a long-run average, like the mean.

Businesses use this constantly. "Can we pay £1.50 per visitor for ads?" Yes, if each visitor is worth £1.80. "A £10 coupon that 15% of 1,000 customers redeem costs about 0.15 × 1,000 × £10 = £1,500."

Try it

An online shop's funnel. Each percentage is "of the people who reached the step before". Change any of them.

Experiments

  1. Check the AND rule: 0.4 × 0.25 × 0.6 × 0.5. Does it match the overall percentage?
  2. Raise Add to basket from 25 to 30 (a 20% improvement). What happens to the overall rate and the revenue per visitor?
  3. Put it back to 25. Now raise View a product from 40 to 48 (also +20%). Compare. What does that tell you about where to improve?
  4. Set any step to 0. Why does everything after it collapse?

Small numbers bounce

A 1-in-1,000 event sounds rare. Across 1,000 stores, though, you'd expect it about once a day somewhere. Across a small group, a rate can swing wildly: 2 sales out of 10 visitors is "20%" but means very little.

How much a rate can bounce, and how many customers you need before you trust it, is the topic of the next three lessons.

Where you'll meet this

In Excel: conversion rate = =B3/B2; whole-funnel rate = =PRODUCT(C2:C5) on a column of step rates; expected value = =rate*value.

In Power BI: the funnel chart visual shows each stage as a bar, with each stage as a share of the first and of the previous one (Microsoft Learn: Funnel charts). A conversion rate is usually a measure.

DAX preview, don't learn it yet: Conversion = DIVIDE([Orders], [Visitors]). In plain words: orders divided by visitors, returning blank instead of an error if there are no visitors.

In interviews: "Our funnel converts 3%. Product wants to redesign the home page, marketing wants to fix checkout. Where would you look?" A strong answer: "Every step multiplies, so a 20% lift anywhere lifts the total by 20%. I'd compare which step is cheapest to improve and where we lose the most people, and check the numbers per step are '% of previous'."

Drawing the line

In Lesson 0010, r told you that distance and time move together. Now draw a line through the dots and read it as a rule: about 9 minutes to start, plus about 2 minutes for every kilometre. Then learn when to stop trusting it.

What you'll calculate

The goal of this lesson is one number: a prediction. That's the line's estimate of Y for an X value you choose. For example, "a 12 km delivery should take about 34 minutes."

ThingWho works it outName
Slope and interceptExcel or Power BI, from the dotsThe line's two settings
The estimated YYou: intercept + slope × XPrediction, also called the predicted value or fitted value, written ŷ ("y-hat")

A prediction isn't always about the future. It's just "what the line expects Y to be at this X". When X is a date and you predict ahead, the prediction is called a forecast, which is the word Excel and Power BI use.

New words
Prediction (ŷ, "y-hat")
The line's estimate of Y for a given X: intercept + slope × X. Also called the predicted or fitted value. A prediction about future dates is a forecast.
Trend line
The straight line that best fits the dots on a scatter plot. Also called the line of best fit, a regression line, or a linear trend.
Intercept
Where the line starts: its value when the across-number is 0. Like "9 minutes at 0 km".
Slope
How much the line climbs (or falls) for each 1 step to the right. The "per unit" number, like "2.1 minutes per km".
Gap (residual)
How far one dot sits above or below the line.
Extrapolation
Using the line to predict outside the range of your data. Risky.
r² (r-squared)
How much of the guessing error the line removes, compared with always guessing the mean. 0 means no better than the mean, and 1 means perfect. For a straight trend line it equals r × r.

The theory: a line is a rule

A scatter plot shows pairs of numbers: one column across (X), one column up (Y). Lesson 0010's r told you whether they move together. A trend line tells you by how much. It's the single straight line that sits closest to all the dots at once.

Every straight trend line has the same shape:

Y ≈ intercept + slope × X

PartDefinition
YThe column on the up-axis: what you want to predict (the outcome).
XThe column across: what you already know (the input).
≈"About". Real dots sit above or below the line, so the line gives an estimate, not an exact value.
Intercept (the start)The line's Y value when X = 0. It's where the line crosses the up-axis.
Slope (the step)How much Y changes for each 1-unit step in X. Its unit is "Y units per X unit".

The slope's sign tells you which way the line goes:

  • Positive slope: the line climbs. More X goes with more Y.
  • Negative slope: the line falls. More X goes with less Y.
  • Zero slope: the line is flat. X tells you nothing about Y.

Using the rule: put an X value in, then multiply first, add second. Slope × X gives the "per unit" part, and adding the intercept gives the prediction (ŷ): the line's estimate of Y at that X. That's the number you'll calculate throughout this lesson:

ŷ = intercept + slope × X. The hat on the y means "estimated by the line", not the real measured Y.

Two formulas for the slope

There are two different jobs, so there are two formulas.

1. Reading the slope off a line that's already drawn: rise ÷ run. Pick any two points on the line. Divide how far it climbs (rise, in Y units) by how far it moves across (run, in X units):

slope = rise ÷ run = (change in Y) ÷ (change in X)

2. Finding the line from the dots: least squares. Tools pick the line whose gaps (how far each dot sits above or below the line) give the smallest total once squared. Write x̄ for the mean of X and ȳ for the mean of Y. That rule works out to:

slope = Σ (x − x̄)(y − ȳ) ÷ Σ (x − x̄)²

intercept = ȳ − slope × x̄

Σ means "add them all up".

Excel and Power BI do formula 2 for you. Your job is to read the slope and intercept they give you and use them. The rest of the lesson applies all of this to real data, then shows when to stop trusting the line.

A start and a step

Every straight line is made of two numbers: where it starts, and how steeply it climbs. The chart below marks both on the delivery line (the faded dots are the same eight deliveries):

Rise ÷ run on this line (formula 1 from the theory): from 10 km to 20 km the line climbs 21 minutes.

slope = 21 min ÷ 10 km = 2.1 min per km

The same shape, Y ≈ intercept + slope × X, reads any trend line:

A slope can be negative, and then the line goes down. If a shop finds Units sold ≈ 500 − 4 × Price, each £1 added to the price goes with about 4 fewer units. A £10 rise goes with about 40 fewer.

Making a prediction

You've already checked the recipe against real deliveries. Its real use is for distances nobody delivered. Same steps, multiply first, then add:

DistanceSumLine says
10 km9 + 2.1 × 10 = 9 + 21about 30 min
15 km9 + 2.1 × 15 = 9 + 31.5about 40 min

There's no 10 km delivery in the data, but the line fills the gap. On the chart, that's going up from 10 km to the line, then across to the time.

Now try 60 km. The sum still works: 9 + 2.1 × 60 = 135 minutes. But the data only covers 3 to 25 km. At 60 km the driver is probably on a motorway or taking a break, so the rule from short city trips may not hold.

Predicting outside your data is called extrapolation. Give the number if someone asks, but say it's a guess. The intercept has the same problem: nobody made a 0 km delivery, so treat "9 minutes" as where the line starts, not a measured fact.

How the line is chosen: the smallest gaps

Why this line and not a slightly steeper one? Look at the gaps: how far each dot sits above or below the line. Statisticians call them residuals.

For our eight deliveries the gaps are tiny (about 4 minutes at most, as the check table showed), so here is a messier week of eight deliveries, where they're easy to see:

The method tools use is called least squares (OpenIntro Statistics, Ch. 8):

  1. Measure each dot's gap to the line.
  2. Square each gap. Gaps above and below then both count, and big misses count extra.
  3. Add the squares up.
  4. Pick the line with the smallest total.

Squaring should feel familiar: standard deviation in Lesson 0008 did the same thing. In practice Excel and Power BI find the line for you.

Under the hood: the slope formula

This is formula 2 from the theory section, now worked through on the deliveries. Here x̄ is the mean distance and ȳ is the mean time:

slope = Σ (x − x̄)(y − ȳ) ÷ Σ (x − x̄)²

intercept = ȳ − slope × x̄

Σ means "add them all up".

In words:

  • Top: for each delivery, how far its distance is from the mean distance, times how far its time is from the mean time. Add them up. This measures how distance and time move together (it's also inside r from Lesson 0010).
  • Bottom: how far each distance is from the mean distance, squared, added up. This measures how spread out the distances are.
  • Top ÷ bottom: how much time moves per unit of distance.

Worked example with the eight deliveries. Mean distance x̄ = 106 ÷ 8 = 13.25 km. Mean time ȳ = 298 ÷ 8 = 37.25 min.

  1. Slope = 878.5 ÷ 411.5 = 2.13 minutes per km.
  2. Intercept = 37.25 − 2.13 × 13.25 = 37.25 − 28.29 = 8.96 minutes.
  3. Rounded, that's the line you've been using: Time ≈ 9 + 2.1 × km. Excel's SLOPE and INTERCEPT give exactly 2.13 and 8.96.

Notice the signs. Short deliveries are below the mean on both distance and time, so minus × minus gives plus. Long ones are above on both, plus × plus. Every row adds up positive, so the slope is positive. If long trips were quicker, the products would be negative and so would the slope.

You won't need to do this by hand at work. Seeing it once shows the slope isn't magic: it's built from the same "distance from the mean" idea as SD and r.

One odd delivery can tilt the line

Because big gaps count extra, one extreme point pulls hard. Suppose a ninth delivery, I, is 22 km but gets stuck in a traffic jam and takes 120 minutes:

One unusual trip changed the "rule" for all the others. That's why you look at the plot first and ask whether an odd point belongs in the data (a one-off jam) or tells you something real.

r²: how far to trust the line

r² answers one question: how much better does the line guess than just using the average?

Imagine someone asks how long a delivery will take. You can guess in two ways:

Without the lineWith the line
What you sayAlways the mean: 37 min9 + 2.1 × km
Typical missabout 15 minabout 2 min

"Typical miss" works like the SD from Lesson 0008: the square root of the average squared miss.

Knowing the distance shrinks your mistakes a lot. r² measures by how much, in three steps:

  1. Square each miss when you always guess the mean (37.25), and add them up: 1,913.5.
  2. Do the same for the line's misses, which are the gaps: 38.0.
  3. r² is the share of the error the line got rid of: 1 − 38.0 ÷ 1,913.5 = 1 − 0.02 = 0.98.

So the line removes about 98% of the guessing error. The 2% left is everything distance can't explain: traffic, stairs, a chatty customer. You'll often hear this read as "98% of the differences in delivery time line up with distance".

The shortcut: for a straight trend line, this always comes out the same as r × r. For the deliveries r = 0.99, and 0.99 × 0.99 ≈ 0.98. That's why r² is always between 0 and 1.

Now compare the eight deliveries with the messier week from the gaps picture. The line comes out almost the same, but the dots don't stay close to it:

In the messy week the line only removes about half the error. A 10 km prediction still says about 30 minutes, but real trips could easily take 15 or 45. A low r² doesn't make the line wrong. It makes it loose: a rough average, not a reliable rule.

r²What it means for your predictions
1The line is perfect: no misses at all.
0.98The line removes almost all the error. Predictions are tight.
0.5The line removes about half. Predictions are rough.
0The line is no better than guessing the average.

So the two parts work together. Slope and intercept give you the prediction ("10 km → about 30 min"). r² tells you how much to trust it: within a couple of minutes, or give or take 15.

Excel and Power BI show r² next to trend lines, usually written R². It doesn't tell you whether you're extrapolating, or whether distance causes the time. Those checks stay separate, and all of Lesson 0010's warnings still apply.

Where you'll meet this

In Excel: right-click the dots on a scatter chart → Add Trendline, then tick "Display equation" and "Display R-squared". Or use formulas. With the distances in A2:A9 and the times in B2:B9:

FormulaGivesHere
=SLOPE(B2:B9, A2:A9)The slope2.13
=INTERCEPT(B2:B9, A2:A9)The intercept8.96
=RSQ(B2:B9, A2:A9)r²0.98
=FORECAST.LINEAR(10, B2:B9, A2:A9)Prediction at 10 km30.3

Watch the order: the Y range (what you predict) comes first, then the X range. Swap them and you get a different, wrong slope (Microsoft Support: SLOPE).

In Power BI: select a scatter or line chart and open the Analytics pane (the magnifying glass icon) to add a trend line. Line charts with a date axis also offer a Forecast option (Microsoft Learn: Analytics pane). The PL-300 exam lists "reference lines, error bars, and forecasting" by name.

In interviews: "Our trend line says sales will hit £2 million next year. Do you trust it?" A strong answer: "It depends how far it reaches past our data and how well the line fits, so I'd check R². I'd also ask what could change, like a new competitor or saturation, since a straight line assumes the past pattern continues."

Lesson 0005 · Stage 4: Growth over time

Which months count?

Year-to-date totals and moving averages: one window that grows, one that slides.

⏱ 15–20 minutes, works on a phone ✎ Mental math is enough 🎯 Win: calculate YTD and a 3-month average, and know when to use each
New words
Running total
A sum that keeps adding each new period to everything before it.
YTD
Year to date: the running total since 1 January. It starts again from zero every new year.
Moving average
The mean (the everyday average) of the last few periods, recalculated each period. Also called a rolling average.
Window
The set of periods that go into one calculation.
Noise
Random ups and downs that don't mean anything, like a rainy week.

The only question: which months go in?

In Lesson 0004 you compared one month with one other month. Today each calculation uses a group of months, called a window.

There are two common windows. The maths inside each is something you already know: a sum, or a mean.

Year to date: a window that grows

The same ice cream shop as last lesson (example figures, k€). September 2026 is selected. The lighter bars are the months inside the window. Tap other months and watch the window grow, then jump back to one bar in January.

Look at the grey note under the sum. The fair way to judge a YTD figure is against the same months last year. Jan–Sep 2026 compared with Jan–Sep 2025 is a YoY comparison of two windows, so the seasons cancel out just like in Lesson 0004.

The YTD trap

In September, never compare YTD with last year's full total. That pits 9 months against 12, so this year always looks behind until December.

Moving average: a window that slides

A different business: an online shop's monthly orders. The bars zigzag up and down, which makes it hard to see if things are improving. The dark line is the 3-month moving average.

Each point on the line is the mean of that month and the two before it. Single good or bad months get averaged with their neighbours, so the noise shrinks and the trend shows. Smoothing like this is one of the oldest tools for finding the trend in a time series (Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, Forecasting: Principles and Practice, §6.2).

The price of smoothing

A moving average reacts late. If orders suddenly crash, the line only drops partly at first, because two older months still hold it up. Use it to see trends, not to catch sudden events.

Where you'll meet this

In Excel: a running total in C2 is =SUM($B$2:B2), copied down. The $ pins the start, so the window grows. A 3-month moving average in row 4 is =AVERAGE(B2:B4), copied down. With no $, both ends move, so the window slides.

In Power BI (preview, nothing to memorise): DAX has a ready-made TOTALYTD function, which means "add up from the start of the year to this date" (DAX docs). Power BI's quick measures can build both a running total and a rolling average for you without typing any DAX.

Practice

Primary source

Read the first part of §6.2 "Moving averages" in Forecasting: Principles and Practice, a free textbook by two leading forecasting researchers. Stop at "Moving averages of moving averages". Their example puts the window in the middle (centred), while business reports usually use the last few months (trailing). The idea is the same.

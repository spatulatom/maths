Lesson 0006 · Stage 4: Growth over time

Growth on growth

Why you can't average yearly growth rates, and the one number that answers "how fast did we grow per year?"

⏱ 15–20 minutes, works on a phone ✎ Mental math for the quiz, Excel for real data 🎯 Win: calculate and explain CAGR, and spot the averaging trap
New words
Compound growth
Growth that builds on last year's result, not on the original starting value. Like interest earning interest.
CAGR
Compound annual growth rate: the single steady yearly rate that gets you from the start value to the end value. Say it "KAG-er".
Growth factor
A percent change written as a multiplier: +10% is ×1.10, −20% is ×0.80.
Exponent
The small raised number: 1.12 means 1.1 × 1.1. In Excel it's written 1.1^2.

The trap: averaging percentages

A shop made 100 in year one, 150 in year two (+50%), and 120 in year three (−20%).

The average of +50% and −20% is +15%. That sounds like a healthy yearly growth rate. Now check it: 100 grown by 15% twice is 100 × 1.15 × 1.15 = 132. But the shop actually ended at 120.

The average was wrong because each year's percentage is calculated on a different base. The −20% came off a bigger number (150) than the +50% was added to (100). You've seen this before: in Lesson 0001, +25% then −20% got you back to where you started.

The fix: multiply, then share it out

Growth stacks by multiplying, so the yearly rate has to undo a multiplication. That's what CAGR does (Corporate Finance Institute):

CAGR = (end ÷ start)1 / periods − 1

In plain words: find the total growth factor (end ÷ start), then ask "what number, multiplied by itself once per period, gives that?"

  1. Total factor: 120 ÷ 100 = 1.2
  2. Periods: 3 years of data, so 2 periods of growth
  3. Share it out: 1.21/2 is the square root of 1.2, which is about 1.095
  4. Back to a percent: 1.095 − 1 = +9.5% per year

Check it by growing it back: 100 × 1.095 × 1.095 ≈ 120. ✓ The averaging shortcut said 15%, but the real yearly rate was under 10%.

Count the gaps, not the years

2021 to 2025 is 5 years of data but only 4 periods of growth. Count the jumps between years, not the years themselves. This is the most common CAGR mistake.

Try it

An online shop's yearly revenue (example figures, k€). The dashed line grows by the simple average every year and overshoots. The solid line grows by the CAGR every year and lands exactly on the last value. Change any number and watch both lines.

Experiments

  1. Set every year to grow by exactly the same percentage, for example 100, 110, 121, 133.1. What happens to the two lines?
  2. Make the middle years wild, like 100, 300, 50, 180. Does the CAGR change? (Only the first and last values matter!)
  3. Can you make the simple average come out lower than the CAGR? (You can't. When growth bounces around, the simple average always overstates it.)
What CAGR hides

CAGR is a smoothed rate. It describes a steady path the business never actually took, and it ignores everything between the first and last year. A year that crashed and recovered looks the same as a calm one (CFI: "Disadvantages of CAGR"). Show the yearly values next to it when the ride was bumpy.

Where you'll meet this

In Excel: with the start in B2 and the end in B6 (4 periods), type =(B6/B2)^(1/4)-1 and format the cell as a percentage. Excel also has a ready-made function, =RRI(4, B2, B6), meaning "periods, start value, end value" (Microsoft Support).

In Power BI (preview, nothing to memorise): there's no built-in CAGR function. A measure uses POWER(end / start, 1 / periods) - 1, where POWER raises a number to an exponent (DAX docs). It's the same formula with different syntax.

In interviews: "Revenue went +50% then −50%. What's the average growth?" is a classic. The answer is not 0%: 100 → 150 → 75, which is a loss.

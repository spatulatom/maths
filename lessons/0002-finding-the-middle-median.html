Finding the middle

How to find a median, and how to tell when it beats the mean.

⏱ 15–20 minutes (+20 in Excel on a long day) ✎ Pen and paper 🎯 Win: find any median by hand and know when to report it

You already have the instinct

In the check, you said the median best describes a typical salary when one director earns far more than everyone else. That's the right call, and it's the reason analysts use medians at all.

What was missing is the mechanics: how to actually find one. It's a three-step recipe, and it never changes.

The recipe

  1. Sort the values from smallest to largest. Skipping this step is the number one mistake.
  2. Count them. Call the count n.
  3. Take the middle.
    • If n is odd, the median is the single middle value.
    • If n is even, there are two middle values. The median is their mean: add them and divide by 2.

Example 1: odd count (the question you skipped)

3, 9, 1, 7, 100 → sorted 1, 3, 7, 9, 100. Five values, so the middle is the 3rd: 7.

Example 2: even count

8, 2, 6, 4 → sorted 2, 4, 6, 8. Four values, so the two middle ones are 4 and 6: (4 + 6) ÷ 2 = 5.

Excel's MEDIAN does exactly this. Microsoft's own example gives 3.5 for six values, "the midway point between the third and fourth numbers" (Excel MEDIAN docs).

Shortcut for long lists

The middle position is (n + 1) ÷ 2. With 9 values that's the 5th. With 10 values it's 5.5, meaning halfway between the 5th and 6th.

Play with it

This strip plots your numbers. The solid line is the median and the dashed line is the mean. Try these three things:

  1. Change 100 to 1000. Which line moves, and which stays put?
  2. Add a sixth number, like 5. Two values light up in the sorted row now. Why?
  3. Delete the 100 entirely. What happens to the gap between the two lines?

What you should notice: the median only cares about order, so an extreme value barely moves it. The mean uses every value's size, so one extreme value pulls it a long way.

Why an analyst cares

When the mean is much bigger than the median, a few very large values are stretching the data. Salaries, house prices, order values, and delivery times often look like this. For data shaped like that, the median is the honest "typical" number, and a big gap between the two is itself worth mentioning in a report.

Power BI has the same function: MEDIAN(Sales[Amount]) in DAX returns the median of a column, and it ignores blanks (DAX MEDIAN docs).

Practice

Work these out on paper before clicking. The last two revisit Lesson 0001, because pulling something back from memory after a gap is what makes it stick.

Long day? Do it in Excel

  1. Type these delivery times (minutes) into A1:A7: 22, 25, 19, 31, 24, 27, 140.
  2. In C1 type =MEDIAN(A1:A7). In C2 type =AVERAGE(A1:A7). Predict both values on paper first, then compare.
  3. Change 140 to 26. Which of the two cells changed a lot?
  4. Add an 8th value in A8 and extend both formulas to A1:A8. Check that the median is now the mean of the two middle values.
  5. Write one sentence you could put in a report: "Typical delivery time is __ minutes; the average is higher because __."

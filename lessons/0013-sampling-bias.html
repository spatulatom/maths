Lesson 0013 · Stage 7: Uncertainty

Who did you ask?

Most numbers come from a sample, not from everyone. Size decides how much a sample wobbles. Bias decides whether it's aimed at the right target, and no amount of size fixes bias.

⏱ 20–30 minutes, works on a phone ✎ No calculation, mostly reasoning 🎯 Win: tell "too small" apart from "biased", and name four common biases
New words
Population
Everyone you want to know about: all customers, all orders.
Sample
The part you actually measured: the 400 who answered the survey.
Random sample
A sample where every member of the population had the same chance of being picked.
Sampling error
The natural wobble between one sample's answer and the truth. Shrinks as the sample grows.
Bias
A systematic lean: the sample keeps missing in the same direction. Doesn't shrink as the sample grows.

Samples are everywhere

You met the population/sample split in Lesson 0008, where STDEV.S was the sample version. In business, samples show up constantly: customer surveys, user tests, a "random 5% of orders" audit, a pilot in three stores.

Even complete data can be a sample of something bigger. This year's orders are a sample of how customers tend to behave, and next year's will differ a little.

Two different ways to be wrong

Sampling error (wobble)Bias (lean)
What it looks likeEach sample lands a bit off, randomly high or lowSamples land off in the same direction
CauseLuck of the drawHow the sample was chosen
Bigger sample?HelpsDoesn't help at all
FixMore dataChange who you sample

Think of darts. Wobble is a spread-out cluster around the bullseye. Bias is a tight cluster in the wrong place.

How much does size help?

The wobble shrinks with the square root of the sample size. To halve it, you need four times as many people. Going from 100 to 400 halves the wobble; going from 100 to 200 only trims it by about 30%.

That's why surveys of around 1,000 people are common: past that, each extra person buys very little extra precision.

Try it

A shop has 5,000 customers. Each "sample" surveys some of them at random and works out their average order value. Each dot is one sample's answer; the green line is the truth.

Experiments

  1. With size 10, press Draw 20 samples. How far from the green line do the dots land? Note the "typically miss by" number.
  2. Switch to 200 and draw 20 again. Then 1000. Is it four times bigger roughly half the miss?
  3. Stay at 1000 and tick Biased survey. Draw 20. The dots are tight, so it looks precise. Where are they centred?
  4. Switch the biased survey to size 10. Is it worse in a different way?

Four biases to name in a meeting

BiasWhat happensBusiness example
SelectionSome groups can't be pickedSurveying app users only, so customers who shop in store are never asked.
Non-responseOnly some kinds of people answerVery happy and very angry customers fill in surveys; the middle stays quiet.
SurvivorshipYou only see who's still aroundAsking current customers why people leave. The ones who left aren't there to ask.
ConvenienceYou ask whoever is easiestTesting a new design on colleagues, who know the product far better than customers do.

The question to ask of any number: who could have ended up in this data, and who couldn't?

Where you'll meet this

In Excel: to take a fair random sample, add a column =RAND(), sort by it, and take the first N rows. Every row has the same chance of ending up near the top.

In Power BI: Power Query's column quality, distribution, and profile tools are based on the first 1,000 rows by default (Microsoft Learn: Data profiling tools). That's a sample, and not a random one: the first rows might all be the oldest orders. Click the status bar text at the bottom to switch to "Column profiling based on entire data set" before trusting min, max, or error counts.

In interviews: "Our survey of 5,000 customers says 92% are satisfied. Thoughts?" A strong answer: "5,000 is plenty for precision, so wobble isn't the worry. Bias is. Who was asked, and who replied? If it went to active app users, we've skipped lapsed customers, and they're the ones most likely to be unhappy."

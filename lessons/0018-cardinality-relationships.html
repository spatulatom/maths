Lesson 0018 · Logic

One to many

How tables relate decides whether your totals are right. One duplicate key in the wrong place, and sales quietly double.

⏱ 25–30 minutes, best on a wider screen ✎ Counting only 🎯 Win: name the "one" and "many" sides, spot double counting, and sketch a star schema
New words
Cardinality
How many rows on one side can match how many on the other: one-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-many.
Unique key
A column where every value appears only once, such as ProductID in a product list.
Fact table
The big table of events: sales, orders, clicks. Many rows, mostly numbers.
Dimension table
A lookup table describing things: products, customers, dates. One row per thing.
Star schema
A fact table in the middle, with dimension tables around it, each linked one-to-many.

One customer, many orders

Each customer appears once in the Customers table. The same customer can appear many times in Orders. That's a one-to-many relationship, and it's the shape of nearly every relationship in a good Power BI model.

CardinalityExampleHow common
One-to-many (1:*)Product → Sales linesThe normal case
One-to-one (1:1)Employee → Employee's payroll recordRare; often the two tables could be one
Many-to-many (*:*)Students ↔ CoursesNeeds care; easy to get wrong totals

The star schema

Power BI works best with a star schema: one fact table (Sales) in the middle, joined one-to-many to dimension tables (Products, Customers, Dates, Stores) around it (Microsoft Learn: Star schema). Drawn out, it looks like a star.

  • Dimensions are the "one" side. You slice and filter by them: "by product", "by month".
  • Facts are the "many" side. You add up their numbers: sum of sales, count of orders.
  • Filters flow from the one side to the many side. Picking "Tea" in a Products slicer narrows the Sales rows to Tea.

How totals double

The "one" side must really be one: each key exactly once. If the product list has P2 twice (say, an old name kept by mistake), every P2 sale finds two partners. A merge copies those sale rows, and the totals inflate.

Power BI protects you in the model: it won't create a one-to-many relationship if the "one" column has duplicates. Power Query merges have no such protection, though, which is why the row-count check from Lesson 0016 matters.

Try it

Sales (many) merged with Products (one). Start with the clean list, then switch to the list with a duplicate key. Pink rows in Products share a key, which breaks the "one" side. Repeats in Sales are normal: that's the "many" side.

Experiments

  1. With Clean product list and Left outer: 6 sales rows in, how many out? Does the total match 205?
  2. Switch to Product list with a duplicate. How many rows now, and what's the total? Which sales were counted twice?
  3. Try Inner on the duplicate list. Does inner join protect you? (No: it's about matching, not uniqueness.)
  4. How would you fix it: change the join, or fix the product list?

Counting rows vs counting things

On the many side, the same customer appears again and again. So "how many customers bought?" is not the number of order rows. You need a distinct count: count each customer once.

QuestionCount
How many orders?Rows in Orders
How many customers ordered?Distinct CustomerIDs in Orders
How many customers do we have?Rows in Customers

Where you'll meet this

In Excel: =COUNTA(UNIQUE(B2:B500)) counts distinct customer IDs. A pivot table can do it too, if you tick "Add this data to the Data Model" and choose Distinct Count.

In Power BI: Model view shows relationships as lines marked 1 and * (Microsoft Learn: Model relationships). PL-300 lists "define a relationship's cardinality and cross-filter direction" and "create a common date table". Both build on this lesson.

DAX preview, don't learn it yet: Customers who bought = DISTINCTCOUNT(Orders[CustomerID]). In plain words: count each customer ID once, however many orders it has.

In interviews: "After adding a Products table, total sales went up by 8%. What happened?" A strong answer: "Probably duplicate ProductIDs in the product table, so some sales rows matched twice in a merge, or the relationship became many-to-many. I'd check the row count before and after, and check whether the key column is unique."

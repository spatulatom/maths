Lesson 0007 · Stage 5: Spread & shape

How far is too far?

Measuring how spread out data is, and a simple rule that tells you when a value counts as an outlier.

⏱ 20–25 minutes, works on a phone ✎ Pen and paper or mental math for the quiz 🎯 Win: find quartiles, the IQR, and outliers by hand, and read a box plot
New words
Spread
How far apart the values are. Also called variability or dispersion.
Range
Largest value minus smallest value.
Quartiles
Three cut points that split sorted data into four equal-sized groups. Q1 is a quarter of the way up, Q2 is the median, and Q3 is three quarters of the way up.
IQR
Interquartile range: Q3 − Q1. The width of the middle half of the data.
Fences
The two cut-off lines for outliers. Anything outside them gets flagged.
Box plot
A chart that draws the quartiles as a box, with lines (called "whiskers") reaching out to the rest of the data. Also called a box-and-whisker chart.

The average isn't the whole story

Two couriers deliver five parcels each. Delivery times in days:

CourierDaysMeanMedian
A4, 5, 5, 5, 655
B1, 2, 5, 8, 955

Same mean (the everyday average), same median. But you'd trust courier A, because its times are bunched together. Courier B might take a day or nine. That difference is spread, and the mean and median can't show it.

Range: quick, but easily fooled

The simplest measure of spread is the range: max − min. Courier A: 6 − 4 = 2. Courier B: 9 − 1 = 8.

The problem is that the range uses only the two most extreme values. One lost parcel that took 40 days would push courier A's range to 36, even though the other four deliveries were fine. It's the same weakness as the mean in Lesson 0002: one extreme value pulls the whole number.

Quartiles: medians of the halves

The fix mirrors the median: ignore the extremes and look at the middle. You already know how to find the middle of a list. Quartiles just do it twice more (OpenIntro Statistics, §2.1).

Here are 11 delivery times, already sorted:

2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 8, 21

  1. Median (Q2): 11 values, so the 6th one: 5.
  2. Split into halves. With an odd count, leave the median out. Lower half: 2, 3, 3, 4, 4. Upper half: 5, 6, 7, 8, 21.
  3. Q1 is the median of the lower half: 3.
  4. Q3 is the median of the upper half: 7.
  5. IQR = Q3 − Q1 = 7 − 3 = 4.

So the middle half of all deliveries took between 3 and 7 days. Notice that the 21 didn't affect the IQR at all. Change it to 210 and the IQR is still 4. That's why the IQR is the spread measure to use for messy, real-world data.

Even count?

With an even number of values, there's no single middle value to leave out. Just cut the list exactly in half. With 8 values, the lower half is the first 4 and the upper half is the last 4.

The 1.5 × IQR rule

On 27 October you asked how far is "far enough" for an outlier. Here's the standard answer. A value is flagged as an outlier if it's more than 1.5 × IQR beyond the box (Lumen Learning: Outliers):

  • Lower fence = Q1 − 1.5 × IQR
  • Upper fence = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR

For the delivery times: 1.5 × 4 = 6. Lower fence: 3 − 6 = −3. Upper fence: 7 + 6 = 13. Nothing is below −3, but 21 is above 13, so it's an outlier.

A rule of thumb, not a law

The 1.5 is a convention statisticians agreed on, not something that comes out of a proof. A flagged value means "go and look", not "delete it". Lumen Learning puts it as: potential outliers "always require further investigation".

Try it: the box plot

The box runs from Q1 to Q3, and the thick line inside it is the median. The whiskers reach out to the furthest values that are still inside the fences. Outliers show up as red dots, and the dashed lines are the fences.

Experiments

  1. Change the 21 to 12. Is it still an outlier? What value is the exact cut-off?
  2. Change the 21 to 210. Watch the range jump while the IQR stays at 4.
  3. Type in courier A (4, 5, 5, 5, 6) and then courier B (1, 2, 5, 8, 9). Compare the width of the boxes.
  4. Change the 2 to a 0. Is it a low outlier? Look at where the lower fence is, and think about why delivery times here can never have a low outlier.

Found an outlier. Now what?

An outlier is a question, not an answer. In real data it's usually one of three things:

  • A mistake: a typo (210 instead of 21), a test order, wrong units. Fix it or remove it, and write down that you did.
  • A real but unusual event: one huge business customer, a parcel lost in a strike. Keep it, but maybe report the median instead of the mean.
  • The actual story: fraud, a broken process, a viral product. Sometimes the outlier is the most important thing in the report.

Where you'll meet this

In Excel: =QUARTILE.INC(A2:A20, 1) gives Q1 and =QUARTILE.INC(A2:A20, 3) gives Q3 (Microsoft Support). Excel uses a slightly different method from the hand method above, so on small lists its answers can be a little off from yours. Both are correct, they just follow different rules. Excel also has a built-in box and whisker chart.

In Power BI (preview, nothing to memorise): a measure for Q1 looks like PERCENTILE.INC(Orders[Days], 0.25), where 0.25 means "25% of the way up" (DAX docs). The PL-300 exam lists "Detect outliers and anomalies" as a skill (study guide), and this rule is the standard starting point.

In interviews: "How would you find outliers in this column?" A strong short answer: "Quartiles, then the 1.5 × IQR fences, and then I'd investigate each flagged value before deciding what to do with it."

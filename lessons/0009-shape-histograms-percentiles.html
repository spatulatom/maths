Lesson 0009 · Stage 5: Spread & shape

What shape is it?

Is your data even on both sides, or lopsided? That's its shape. Learn the three shapes, how to see them with a histogram, and how percentiles describe a long tail.

⏱ 20–25 minutes, works on a phone ✎ Mostly reasoning, a little counting 🎯 Win: read a histogram, name its shape, and say which average and spread to report
New words
Distribution
How the values are spread across their range: where they pile up and where they thin out. Its "shape".
Symmetric
Even on both sides of the middle, like a bell. The mean and median sit together.
Skewed
Lopsided. Right-skewed means a long tail of big values on the right. Left-skewed means a long tail on the left.
Histogram
A chart of a distribution. Each bar is one range of values, and its height counts how many values fall in that range.
Bin
One of those ranges, like a labelled bucket: "€20 up to €40". Every value drops into exactly one bin. All bins in a histogram are equally wide.
Bin width
How wide each bin is, for example €20. You choose it, and the choice changes how the histogram looks.
Percentile
The value with a given percentage of the data at or below it. P90 has 90% of values at or below it.

This is the third branch of the map from Lesson 0007: centre, spread, and now shape. The lesson has three parts, and each plays a different role:

  • Shapes are the answer you're looking for: symmetric, right-skewed or left-skewed.
  • Histograms are the tool you use to see the shape.
  • Percentiles put numbers on the shape, especially on a long tail.

Shape is what tells you which centre and spread to report.

Three shapes to recognise

ShapeLooks likeMean vs medianTypical business data
SymmetricOne hump in the middle, even on both sides (bell-shaped)About equalHeights, measurement errors, test scores
Right-skewedHump on the left, long tail to the rightMean above medianOrder values, incomes, house prices, delivery times, website session lengths
Left-skewedLong tail to the left, hump on the rightMean below medianScores on an easy exam, product ratings out of 5

Why does the mean move? It gets pulled toward the tail, because a few huge values add a lot to the total. The median only cares about the middle position, so it stays put (OpenIntro Statistics, §2.1). This is the same effect you saw with one outlier in Lesson 0002, spread across a whole tail.

Most business money data is right-skewed

Amounts can't go below zero, but a few customers, deals or houses can be enormous. That's why "average salary" usually sounds higher than what a typical person earns. When you see money, expect a right skew and check the median.

Seeing the shape: histograms

You can't see a shape in a list of 10,000 numbers. A histogram is the tool that shows it: it groups values into equal-width bins and draws one bar per bin showing how many values landed there. The curves in the pictures above are just histograms with the bars smoothed out.

So one bar is not one value. One bar is a range of values, and its height is how many values fell in that range.

What's a bin?

A bin is a bucket for a range of values. Picture sorting post into pigeonholes labelled "0–5 days", "5–10 days", and so on. Every value drops into exactly one pigeonhole, and then you count what's in each.

  • Bins cover the whole range with no gaps or overlaps. They start at or below the smallest value and run past the largest.
  • The labels hide a detail. "5–10" looks as if it includes both 5 and 10, but it means "5 up to, not including, 10". A 10 goes in the next bin, 10–15.
  • Bin width = how wide each bucket is (5 days here). Number of bins ≈ (largest − smallest) ÷ bin width, so wider bins mean fewer bars.
  • Binning isn't only for histograms. Age bands (18–24, 25–34), order-size bands (small, medium, large) and salary bands are all bins. Reports use them to turn a long list of numbers into a few groups people can compare.

The bars touch, because the bins sit side by side on a number line. Empty bins stay in the chart as gaps: they're part of the shape.

Values on an edge: 5 sits exactly where 0–5 ends and 5–10 starts. Following the "up to, not including" rule, it goes in the bin that starts there, 5–10. The chart in "Try it" does the same. Some tools put edge values in the bin below instead, so don't be surprised if counts shift by one.

Histogram or bar chart?

Drawing one bar per value gives you a bar chart, not a histogram. It shows each delivery's own time, but not the shape. The shape only appears once you count values per range.

Choosing the bin width

The same data can look quite different depending on the bin width, so it's worth trying a few:

  • Too wide: everything piles into one or two bars, and you can't see a shape.
  • Too narrow: close to one bar per distinct value, with gaps and spikes that are just noise.
  • About right: roughly 5–20 bars, with round-number edges (€0–20, €20–40) so people can read them easily.

There's no single correct width. Try two or three and pick the one where the shape is clear and doesn't change much if you nudge the width a little.

Histograms in Power BI

Power BI has no built-in "histogram" visual. You build one in two steps (Microsoft Learn: grouping and binning):

  1. Make the bins. In the Data pane, right-click the numeric field (say, Delivery Days) → New group. Set Group type to Bin and choose a bin size, such as 5. Power BI adds a new column, "Delivery Days (bins)".
  2. Count per bin. Add a column chart. Put the bins column on the X-axis and a count of the deliveries on the Y-axis.

That's the PL-300 skill "Use grouping, binning, and clustering" in action. Changing the bin size in the group settings is how you try different widths.

Putting numbers on the shape: percentiles

Percentiles aren't a fourth shape. They mark positions in the sorted data, which makes them handy for measuring how long a tail is.

You've already met three percentiles without the name: Q1 = P25, median = P50, Q3 = P75. Any other percentage works the same way.

Businesses love P90 and P95 because they describe the bad-but-normal case. "Median delivery is 3 days" sounds great. But "P95 delivery is 9 days" tells you that 1 order in 20 takes 9 days or longer, and those are the customers who complain.

In Excel: =PERCENTILE.INC(B2:B500, 0.9). The second argument is a fraction from 0 to 1, so 0.9 means P90 (Microsoft Support).

Working one out by hand

It's the same idea as finding the median: sort, find a position, read the value. This is the method PERCENTILE.INC uses.

  1. Sort the values from smallest to largest.
  2. Find the position: position = 1 + p × (n − 1). Here p is the percentile as a fraction (0.9 for P90) and n is how many values you have.
  3. Read the value. If the position is a whole number, take the value there. If it falls between two positions, go that fraction of the way between their two values.

Here are the 11 delivery times from Lesson 0007, sorted:

Position1234567891011
Days233445567821
  • P90: position = 1 + 0.9 × 10 = 10. The 10th value is 8, so P90 = 8 days.
  • P95: position = 1 + 0.95 × 10 = 10.5, halfway between the 10th value (8) and the 11th (21). So 8 + 0.5 × (21 − 8) = 14.5 days.
  • Check with P50: position = 1 + 0.5 × 10 = 6. The 6th value is 5, which is the median, as it should be.

A percentile doesn't have to be one of your data values: P95 is 14.5 even though no delivery took 14.5 days. Notice also how far the single 21-day delivery pulls P95. That's the tail showing up in the number.

At work you'll let PERCENTILE.INC do this, in Excel or in DAX. Doing it by hand once shows you what the number means, and why tools using other methods can give slightly different answers, just as with quartiles in Lesson 0007.

Experiments

  1. Change the bin width to 5, then to 50. Same data, but does the shape look the same? Bin width is a choice, and a bad one can hide the shape.
  2. Delete the 120 and 160. Which line moves more, the mean or the median?
  3. Set the percentile to 50. What do you notice? Then try 25 and 75. (They may differ slightly from your hand-calculated quartiles, for the same reason as in Lesson 0007.)
  4. Type in something symmetric, like 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, with bin width 1. Where do the mean and median sit?

Where you'll meet this

In Excel: Insert → Insert Statistic Chart → Histogram. Excel picks a bin width automatically, and you can change it under Format Axis (Microsoft Support: Create a histogram).

In Power BI: there's no one-click histogram. Instead you create bins on a numeric column (right-click the field → New group, set the bin size) and then put the bins on a column chart (Microsoft Learn: Grouping and binning). "Use grouping, binning, and clustering" is a listed PL-300 skill (study guide). DAX preview, nothing to memorise: PERCENTILE.INC(Orders[Value], 0.9).

In interviews: "Our average order value went up 20%. Is that good news?" A strong answer: "Maybe. Order values are usually right-skewed, so a few huge orders could have moved the mean while the typical order didn't change. I'd check the median and the histogram."

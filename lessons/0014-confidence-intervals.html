Lesson 0014 · Stage 7: Uncertainty

Give or take

A sample gives you one number. A confidence interval adds the honest part: "…give or take this much".

⏱ 20–30 minutes, works on a phone ✎ Light arithmetic: one square root, one multiply 🎯 Win: read and build a rough 95% interval, and know when "52%" isn't really a majority
New words
Estimate
Your best single guess from a sample, such as the sample's average.
Margin of error
The "give or take" part: how far the truth could plausibly be from the estimate.
Confidence interval
Estimate ± margin of error, written as a range: "£37 to £47".
Standard error (SE)
How much the estimate typically wobbles from sample to sample. For an average: SD ÷ √n.
Error bars
Lines on a chart showing an interval around each value.

One sample, one number, plus a range

In Lesson 0013, every sample landed a bit off the truth. In real life you get one sample and can't see the truth. A confidence interval puts a range around your one answer that will usually contain the truth.

95% interval ≈ estimate ± 2 × SE,   where for an average SE = SD ÷ √n

The "2" comes from the 68–95–99.7 rule in Lesson 0008. Sample averages form a bell shape around the truth, so about 95% of them land within 2 SE of it. (Tools use 1.96, or a slightly bigger number for small samples. 2 is close enough to read results.)

A worked example

  1. You survey 100 customers. Their average order is £42, with SD £25.
  2. SE = 25 ÷ √100 = 25 ÷ 10 = £2.50.
  3. Margin = 2 × 2.50 = £5.
  4. 95% interval: 42 − 5 to 42 + 5 = £37 to £47.

In a report: "Average order value is about £42 (95% CI: £37–£47)."

What "95%" really means

It's about the method, not about this one interval. If you repeated the survey many times and built an interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the truth. About 1 in 20 would miss.

For everyday speech, "we're fairly confident the true average is between £37 and £47" is fine.

Try it

Same 5,000 customers as last lesson. Now each sample draws its interval as a line. Red ones missed the true average.

Experiments

  1. At size 50, press Draw 20 samples a few times. Roughly what share of intervals catch the green line? Is it near 95%?
  2. Switch to 1000. The intervals get much shorter. Do they still catch the truth about 95% of the time?
  3. Size 10: wide intervals, and a few more misses than 5%. (Small samples from skewed data are where the "2 × SE" shortcut gets rough.)
  4. Tick Biased survey at 1000. Short, confident-looking intervals that almost all miss. An interval only covers wobble, never bias.

Percentages: the 1 ÷ √n shortcut

For a survey percentage, a handy worst-case margin is 1 ÷ √n. With 1,000 people: 1 ÷ √1000 ≈ 0.03, so about ± 3 points. That's why polls of 1,000 people quote "± 3%".

So "52% prefer the new design" from 1,000 people means somewhere around 49% to 55%. That range includes 50%, so you can't honestly call it a majority.

Comparing two intervals

Store A's average basket: £40–£48. Store B's: £70–£80. The intervals don't overlap, so B really is higher. If they overlap a lot, such as £40–£48 and £44–£52, you can't claim a difference from this data. For anything in between, use a proper test, which is the next lesson.

Where you'll meet this

In Excel: =CONFIDENCE.T(0.05, STDEV.S(B2:B101), COUNT(B2:B101)) returns the margin, not the interval (Microsoft Support: CONFIDENCE.T). 0.05 means 95% confidence. Subtract it from and add it to the average to get the two ends.

In Power BI: the PL-300 study guide lists "error bars" among the analytics features (Microsoft Learn: PL-300 study guide). On line and bar charts, you add them from the Analytics pane, using measures for the upper and lower bound. Power BI draws them; you supply the interval.

In interviews: "Version B's survey score is 52% vs 50% for A, from 1,000 people each. Did B win?" A strong answer: "Each has a margin of about ± 3 points, so the ranges overlap heavily. I wouldn't call it yet. I'd run a proper significance test or collect more responses."

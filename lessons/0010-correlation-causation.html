Lesson 0010 · Stage 6: Relationships

Moving together

Until now you described one column at a time. Now put two columns side by side and ask: when one goes up, does the other? A scatter plot shows it, the number r measures it, and "causes" is the trap to avoid.

⏱ 25–30 minutes, works on a phone ✎ No calculation, all reasoning 🎯 Win: read r, spot when it lies, and challenge a "causes" claim in an interview
New words
Paired data
Two numbers that belong to the same item, like one delivery's distance and its time. In a table, they're two cells in the same row.
Scatter plot
A chart with one dot per item (per row), placed by its two numbers: one across, one up.
Correlation
How closely two columns move together, row by row, in a straight-line pattern.
r
The correlation coefficient: one number from −1 to +1 that measures correlation. Say "r equals 0.8".
Causation
One thing actually making the other happen. Much harder to show than correlation.
Confounder
A hidden third factor that drives both numbers and makes them look connected. Also called a lurking variable.

The first branch is the centre, spread, shape map, and it still applies: each column has its own centre, spread and shape. Statisticians call one-column analysis univariate and two-column analysis bivariate.

How statistics writes it: one list of pairs, not two sets

One column is a list of n numbers: x1, x2, …, xn. The small number is the row, so x4 is the 4th delivery's distance. (It's a list, not a "set": in maths a set ignores repeats, and data like 3, 3, 4, 4 needs every one.)

Two columns are not two separate lists. They're one list of pairs: (x1, y1), (x2, y2), …, (xn, yn). Each pair shares the same small number, which means the same row. Each pair is also a point on a graph, (across, up), and that's exactly the scatter plot below.

The formula for r works through the pairs, always combining xi with the yi from the same row. Two separate sets would lose who goes with whom, which is exactly the shuffle problem further down.

Statistics textbooks organise data as a table too. OpenIntro Statistics (§1.2) calls it a data matrix: one row per case, one column per variable. So thinking in columns isn't just a spreadsheet habit. It's how statistics itself lays out data.

You need many pairs. One pair is one dot, and one dot can't show a pattern.

One row, one dot: the scatter plot

To see whether two columns move together, draw every row as a dot. Place it across by one column (distance) and up by the other (time):

The dots drift up and to the right: longer distances go with longer times. That drift is what correlation measures.

Both time columns hold exactly the same eight numbers. So they have the same mean (37.25 min), the same median (36.5 min), the same SD and the same histogram. Every measure from Lessons 0002–0009 gives the same answer for both.

But the relationship is gone: r drops from 0.99 to about 0. Correlation measures which value sits next to which, so you can never work it out from either column on its own.

That's also why sorting just one column in Excel is a disaster. Every row silently gets the wrong partner, and any correlation you calculate afterwards is meaningless.

r: direction and strength in one number

The correlation coefficient r compresses that drift into a single number (OpenIntro Statistics, Ch. 8):

  • The sign gives the direction. Positive means they rise together. Negative means one rises as the other falls.
  • The size gives the strength. +1 or −1 means the dots sit perfectly on a straight line. 0 means no straight-line pattern at all.
|r| (size, ignoring the sign)Rough reading
0.7 to 1Strong
0.4 to 0.7Moderate
0.1 to 0.4Weak
below 0.1Little or none

These cut-offs are a common rule of thumb, not a standard. Different fields draw the lines in different places.

So r = −0.9 is stronger than r = +0.6. The minus sign only tells you the direction.

Two ways r can lie

  1. It only sees straight lines. Dots forming a perfect U-shape can have r ≈ 0, even though the two numbers are clearly related. r measures straight-line relationships only.
  2. One point can fake it. A shapeless cloud plus one extreme dot far away can produce r = 0.85. The single point does all the work.

Both traps have the same fix: always look at the scatter plot before you trust r. It's the same habit as "look at the shape first" from Lesson 0009.

Correlation is not causation

Suppose shops that spend more on ads have higher sales, with r = 0.8. That does not mean the ads caused the sales. Before believing "X causes Y", rule out three other explanations:

ExplanationWhat's really going onExample
ConfounderA third factor drives bothIce cream sales and sunburns rise together. Neither causes the other; sunny weather drives both.
Reverse causeY causes X, not the other way roundShops that run more discounts have lower sales. Do discounts hurt sales? More likely the arrow points the other way: weak sales make managers discount.
CoincidenceTwo unrelated series happen to matchTyler Vigen's Spurious Correlations collects hundreds of these, such as cheese consumption tracking deaths from bedsheet tangling.

In the ads example, a likely confounder is store size. Big stores have bigger ad budgets and more customers. The usual way to actually test causation is a controlled experiment, such as an A/B test. You'll meet those in stage 7.

Which column is the cause?

r can't tell you. It's symmetric: swap the two columns and you get exactly the same number, so =CORREL(A, B) equals =CORREL(B, A). It only measures that they move together, not which way an arrow points, or whether there's an arrow at all.

The scatter plot doesn't decide it either. By habit you put the suspected cause on the x-axis (across), but that's your choice, not something the data proves.

So you judge the direction from outside the numbers. Here are four clues, using the deliveries from the start of the lesson:

ClueAskDeliveries
TimingWhich happens first? A cause comes before its effect.The route is fixed before the drive starts.
What can change whatCould Y possibly change X?A slow delivery can't make the road longer.
MechanismIs there a believable "how"?More kilometres means more time behind the wheel.
ExperimentChange X on purpose. Does Y follow?Give some drivers shorter routes and compare their times.

The first three make a direction likely. Only an experiment shows it. And check the confounder row above first: if a third factor drives both, neither column is the cause.

Words to use at work

Say "is associated with" or "moves with", not "drives" or "causes", unless there was an experiment. "Stores with higher ad spend tend to have higher sales (r = 0.8)" is accurate. "Ads drive sales" is a claim you can't back up from this data.

Where you'll meet this

In Excel: =CORREL(A2:A13, B2:B13) returns r for two columns of the same length, matched row by row (Microsoft Support: CORREL). For a scatter plot, use Insert → Scatter.

In Power BI: the scatter chart visual plots one dot per category, using two measures (Microsoft Learn: Scatter charts). For r itself, Power BI's quick measures include a ready-made "Correlation coefficient" (Microsoft Learn: Quick measures). You can also add a trend line, which is the topic of the next lesson.

In interviews: "Ad spend and sales correlate at 0.8. Should we double the ad budget?" A strong answer: "Not from this alone. Bigger stores might simply spend more and sell more. I'd check for confounders like store size, and ideally run a test: raise ads in some stores, not others, and compare."

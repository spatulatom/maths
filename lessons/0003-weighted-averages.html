Lesson 0003 · Stage 3: Averages

Don't average averages

When groups are different sizes, the big groups should count for more.

⏱ 20 minutes (+20 in Excel on a long day) ✎ Pen and paper 🎯 Win: combine group averages correctly, in your head and in Excel
New words
Group
A slice of your data: one store, one month, one class.
Weight
How much a value should count. Usually it's the group's size.
Weighted mean
A mean where bigger groups count for more. You'll often hear it called a "weighted average".
Total
Everything added up. For a group it's count × mean: 100 orders × €10 = €1,000.
Measure
A Power BI calculation (written in DAX) that recalculates for whatever slice the report is showing.

The trap you skipped

In the check, Store A had 100 orders averaging €10, and Store B had 300 orders averaging €20. The tempting answer is (10 + 20) ÷ 2 = €15.

That treats both stores as equal, but Store B has three times as many orders. Three-quarters of all orders were €20 orders, so the true answer must sit much closer to 20 than to 10.

The fix: go back to totals

A mean is total ÷ count. So to combine groups, rebuild the totals first, then divide once:

StoreOrders (count)Mean orderTotal = count × mean
A100€10€1,000
B300€20€6,000
Both400€7,000

Weighted mean = €7,000 ÷ 400 = €17.50.

The rule

Weighted mean = sum of (weight × value) ÷ sum of weights. Here the weights are order counts. "Total revenue ÷ total orders" is exactly the same calculation in plain business language.

Averaging the averages only works when every group is the same size. Then all the weights are equal, and both methods give the same answer.

Play with it

The dot sizes show how many orders each store has. The solid line is the weighted mean and the dashed line is the naive average of the averages. Try these:

  1. Set both stores to 200 orders. The two lines meet. Why?
  2. Set Store A to 5 orders. Where does the weighted mean go now?
  3. Set Store B to 10,000 orders. What would the naive answer tell your manager, and how wrong would it be?

The pattern: the weighted mean is pulled toward the group with the most weight. The naive average always sits exactly halfway, whatever the sizes.

Where you'll meet this

In Excel

With counts in B2:B3 and means in C2:C3:

=SUMPRODUCT(B2:B3, C2:C3) / SUM(B2:B3)

SUMPRODUCT multiplies each pair (100×10, 300×20) and adds the results, so it builds the total for you. Dividing by SUM of the counts finishes the job. This is Microsoft's own recommended pattern (Excel weighted average).

In Power BI (preview, nothing to memorise)

DAX is Power BI's formula language. You'll learn it properly in the Coursera course. For now, read it like an Excel formula: SUM(Sales[Revenue]) means "add up the Revenue column of the Sales table".

This is why a good "average order value" measure looks like this:

Avg Order Value = DIVIDE( SUM(Sales[Revenue]), SUM(Sales[Orders]) )

It divides totals, so it is automatically weighted, and it stays correct in every row and in the grand-total row. DIVIDE is DAX's safe division: it returns blank instead of an error when the bottom number is 0 (DAX DIVIDE docs).

Real-world trap

If someone hands you a spreadsheet that only has per-store averages and no counts, you can't combine them correctly. The first question to ask is: "How many orders are behind each average?"

Practice

Paper first, then click. The last two questions revisit earlier lessons.

Long day? Do it in Excel

  1. Build this table in A1:C4 with headers Region / Customers / Avg spend: North 1,200 / €45, South 300 / €80, West 500 / €60.
  2. Predict on paper first: will the overall mean spend be above or below the naive (45 + 80 + 60) ÷ 3 = €61.67? Why?
  3. In E1: =AVERAGE(C2:C4). In E2: =SUMPRODUCT(B2:B4,C2:C4)/SUM(B2:B4).
  4. Add a column D = =B2*C2 (totals), then check =SUM(D2:D4)/SUM(B2:B4) matches E2.
  5. Change North to 10 customers and watch E2 move while E1 stays put.

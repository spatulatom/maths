Drawing the line

Trend lines turn a cloud of dots into a rule of thumb: "each extra £1 of this goes with about £3 of that". Here's how to read one, and when to stop trusting it.

⏱ 20–30 minutes, works on a phone ✎ Light arithmetic: one multiply, one add 🎯 Win: read a trend line equation, make a safe prediction, and explain r²
New words
Trend line
The straight line that best fits the dots on a scatter plot. Also called the line of best fit, a regression line, or a linear trend.
Slope
How much the line goes up (or down) for each 1 step to the right. The "per unit" number.
Intercept
Where the line crosses the up-axis: the value the line gives when the across-number is 0.
Extrapolation
Using the line to predict outside the range of your data. Risky.
r² (r-squared)
r multiplied by itself. Roughly, the share of the ups and downs in Y that the line accounts for.

From r to a line

In Lesson 0010, r told you whether two numbers move together. A trend line tells you by how much. It's the one straight line that sits closest to all the dots at once.

Tools pick the line by "least squares": they measure how far each dot sits above or below the line, square those gaps, and choose the line that makes the total as small as possible (OpenIntro Statistics, Ch. 8). Squaring should feel familiar: standard deviation in Lesson 0008 did the same thing.

Reading the equation

Every straight trend line can be written as:

Y ≈ intercept + slope × X

Take a shop's 12 months of data, with both numbers in £ thousands. The line comes out as Sales ≈ 30 + 3 × Ad spend.

PartValuePlain words
Slope3Each extra £1k of ad spend goes with about £3k more sales, on average.
Intercept30Where the line would be at £0 ad spend: about £30k of sales.
Prediction30 + 3 × 20 = 90In a month with £20k of ads, the line expects about £90k of sales.

A negative slope means the line goes down. If Units ≈ 500 − 4 × Price, every £1 added to the price goes with about 4 fewer units sold. A £10 rise goes with about 40 fewer.

Three cautions

  1. Don't predict far outside your data. The shop's ad spend ran from £8k to £40k. Asking the line about £200k is extrapolation: ads may stop working long before then. The same goes for the intercept. Nobody tested £0 of ads, so treat "30" as a line setting, not a fact.
  2. The slope is a pattern, not a promise. "Slope = 3" does not mean every extra £1k of ads earns £3k. All of Lesson 0010's warnings still apply: busy months could have both more ads and more sales.
  3. One odd point can tilt the whole line. Just as one point could fake r, one extreme month can drag the line. Look at the plot.

r²: how well the line fits

r² is just r × r, so it's always between 0 and 1. If r = 0.9, then r² = 0.81. A common reading is that about 81% of the ups and downs in Y line up with the trend line, and the other 19% is everything else.

Excel and Power BI both show r² next to trend lines, usually written as R². Higher means the dots hug the line more closely. It says nothing about cause.

Experiments

  1. Predict sales at ad spend 25. Check it by hand: 30.6 + 3 × 25.
  2. Now predict at 0, then at 100. Why does the box warn you both times?
  3. Open Plus one odd month. One month had £45k of ads but only £40k of sales. What happens to the slope and to r²?

Where you'll meet this

In Excel: right-click the dots on a scatter chart → Add Trendline, then tick "Display equation" and "Display R-squared". Or use formulas:

FormulaGives
=SLOPE(B2:B13, A2:A13)The slope
=INTERCEPT(B2:B13, A2:A13)The intercept
=RSQ(B2:B13, A2:A13)r²
=FORECAST.LINEAR(20, B2:B13, A2:A13)The line's prediction at X = 20

Watch the order: the Y range (what you predict) comes first, then the X range. Swap them and you get a different, wrong slope (Microsoft Support: SLOPE).

In Power BI: select a scatter or line chart and open the Analytics pane (the magnifying glass icon) to add a trend line. Line charts with a date axis also offer a Forecast option (Microsoft Learn: Analytics pane). The PL-300 exam lists "reference lines, error bars, and forecasting" by name.

In interviews: "Our trend line says sales will hit £2 million next year. Do you trust it?" A strong answer: "It depends how far it reaches past our data and how well the line fits, so I'd check R². I'd also ask what could change, like a new competitor or saturation, since a straight line assumes the past pattern continues."

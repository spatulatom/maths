Lesson 0010 · Stage 6: Relationships

Moving together

Scatter plots, the correlation number r, and the three other explanations to rule out before anyone says "X causes Y".

⏱ 20–25 minutes, works on a phone ✎ No calculation, all reasoning 🎯 Win: read r, spot when it lies, and challenge a "causes" claim in an interview
New words
Scatter plot
A chart with one dot per item (a store, a month, a customer), placed by two numbers: one across, one up.
Correlation
How closely two numbers move together in a straight-line pattern.
r
The correlation coefficient: one number from −1 to +1 that measures correlation. Say "r equals 0.8".
Causation
One thing actually making the other happen. Much harder to show than correlation.
Confounder
A hidden third factor that drives both numbers and makes them look connected. Also called a lurking variable.

One dot per thing

Every chart so far showed one number per item. A scatter plot shows two. For example, each dot is a store, placed across by its advertising spend and up by its sales. If the dots drift up and to the right, stores that spend more also tend to sell more.

r: direction and strength in one number

The correlation coefficient r compresses that drift into a single number (OpenIntro Statistics, Ch. 8):

  • The sign gives the direction. Positive means they rise together. Negative means one rises as the other falls.
  • The size gives the strength. +1 or −1 means the dots sit perfectly on a straight line. 0 means no straight-line pattern at all.
|r| (size, ignoring the sign)Rough reading
0.7 to 1Strong
0.4 to 0.7Moderate
0.1 to 0.4Weak
below 0.1Little or none

These cut-offs are a common rule of thumb, not a standard. Different fields draw the lines in different places.

So r = −0.9 is stronger than r = +0.6. The minus sign only tells you the direction.

Two ways r can lie

  1. It only sees straight lines. Dots forming a perfect U-shape can have r ≈ 0, even though the two numbers are clearly related. r measures straight-line relationships only.
  2. One point can fake it. A shapeless cloud plus one extreme dot far away can produce r = 0.85. The single point does all the work.

Both traps have the same fix: always look at the scatter plot before you trust r. It's the same habit as "look at the shape first" from Lesson 0009.

Try it

Pick a pattern and read r. Then tap inside the chart to add your own points.

Experiments

  1. Go through the first four patterns. Which has the biggest |r|? Is "strong negative" weaker than "strong positive"?
  2. Open U-shape. The pattern is obvious, so why is r about 0?
  3. Open Shapeless cluster (r ≈ 0). Now tap once in the far top-right corner. What happens to r?
  4. Open Strong positive and add three points in the bottom-right corner. How fast does r drop?

Correlation is not causation

Suppose shops that spend more on ads have higher sales, with r = 0.8. That does not mean the ads caused the sales. Before believing "X causes Y", rule out three other explanations:

ExplanationWhat's really going onExample
ConfounderA third factor drives bothIce cream sales and sunburns rise together. Neither causes the other; sunny weather drives both.
Reverse causeY causes X, not the other way roundFires with more firefighters cause more damage? No: bigger fires get more firefighters sent.
CoincidenceTwo unrelated series happen to matchTyler Vigen's Spurious Correlations collects hundreds of these, such as cheese consumption tracking deaths from bedsheet tangling.

In the ads example, a likely confounder is store size. Big stores have bigger ad budgets and more customers. The usual way to actually test causation is a controlled experiment, such as an A/B test. You'll meet those in stage 7.

Words to use at work

Say "is associated with" or "moves with", not "drives" or "causes", unless there was an experiment. "Stores with higher ad spend tend to have higher sales (r = 0.8)" is accurate. "Ads drive sales" is a claim you can't back up from this data.

Where you'll meet this

In Excel: =CORREL(A2:A13, B2:B13) returns r for two columns of the same length (Microsoft Support: CORREL). For a scatter plot, use Insert → Scatter.

In Power BI: the scatter chart visual plots one dot per category, using two measures (Microsoft Learn: Scatter charts). There's no one-click r, but you can add a trend line, which is the topic of the next lesson.

In interviews: "Ad spend and sales correlate at 0.8. Should we double the ad budget?" A strong answer: "Not from this alone. Bigger stores might simply spend more and sell more. I'd check for confounders like store size, and ideally run a test: raise ads in some stores, not others, and compare."
